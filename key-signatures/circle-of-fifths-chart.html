Circle of Fifths Chart

The circle of fifths is the single most useful diagram in music theory. It shows all 12 major keys arranged so that each key is a perfect fifth above its neighbour — and it tells you instantly how many sharps or flats any key signature contains.

How to Read It

Moving clockwise around the circle, each key is a perfect fifth higher and gains one sharp. Moving counter-clockwise, each key is a perfect fifth lower and gains one flat.

C major sits at the top — it has no sharps or flats. G is one step clockwise (one sharp). D is two steps clockwise (two sharps), and so on.

The Order of Sharps and Flats

Sharps always appear on the staff in the same order: F C G D A E B. A useful mnemonic: Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle.

Flats appear in the reverse order: B E A D G C F. The same sentence backwards: Battle Ends And Down Goes Charles' Father.

Relative Minor Keys

Every major key has a relative minor that shares the same key signature. The relative minor starts on the 6th degree of the major scale — or equivalently, three semitones (a minor third) below the major tonic.

  • C major → A minor (0 accidentals)
  • G major → E minor (1 sharp)
  • D major → B minor (2 sharps)
  • F major → D minor (1 flat)
  • B♭ major → G minor (2 flats)

Quick-Reference Tip

To identify a sharp key signature: the last sharp on the staff is the 7th degree (the leading tone). The key is one semitone above it. For example, if the last sharp is F♯, the key is G major.

To identify a flat key signature: the second-to-last flat is the name of the key. For example, if you see B♭ and E♭, the key is B♭ major. (Exception: one flat = F major.)
